# From Professional Expertise to AI-Native Development: My Journey with Codex

29 October 2026

I never set out to become a software developer.

I'm a business director with a professional background spanning law, property litigation, housing, leadership and homelessness.

Throughout my career, I've worked with complex systems, legislation, operational processes and the challenges of turning professional requirements into functioning software.

I've also spent a considerable amount of time working with software developers, trying to explain how those systems should work.

What I hadn't appreciated was that AI might give me the opportunity to start building them myself.

## From explaining ideas to building them

I've commissioned software development before, including a legal case-management system.

One of the biggest challenges was translating years of professional knowledge into instructions a developer could implement.

It's not simply a matter of explaining what a button should do.

You need to explain the legislation behind a process, why a particular decision matters, what happens in unusual circumstances, how one transaction affects another and why apparently minor exceptions can have significant consequences.

That knowledge can take years to acquire and countless hours to communicate.

And even when everyone believes they've understood one another, the finished software doesn't always behave as expected.

AI-assisted development has fundamentally changed that experience for me.

Instead of repeatedly explaining an idea and waiting for someone else to interpret it, I can work directly with ChatGPT and Codex, test my thinking, examine the results and refine the application as my understanding develops.

The feedback loop is dramatically shorter.

That doesn't eliminate the need for experienced engineers. But it changes my relationship with the development process.

I'm no longer simply commissioning software. I'm actively developing it.

## The extraordinary power of a working prototype

Perhaps my biggest discovery has been the difference between explaining an ambitious idea and showing someone a working prototype.

Some of the systems I'm developing involve complicated relationships between legislation, financial circumstances, operational decisions and human needs.

Explaining those concepts in a meeting can be difficult. Even when people understand the problem, they may struggle to imagine the proposed solution.

But put a coherent, interactive prototype in front of them, and something changes.

Suddenly, they can see the process, follow the decisions, question the assumptions and imagine how it might work in their own organisation.

An abstract idea becomes something people can understand, challenge and help improve.

My prototypes have already attracted interest from senior decision-makers outside my organisation, including those working in public services.

That has been enormously encouraging.

It doesn't mean the applications are finished, endorsed or proven effective. But it demonstrates how valuable a working prototype can be in communicating an ambitious idea.

And creating those prototypes myself has allowed me to explore possibilities that might otherwise have remained on paper.

## Building ambitious software

My current projects span several areas of housing and public services.

I'm developing a replacement property-management system, an AI-assisted homelessness prevention platform, housing-assessment applications and supported-housing research tools.

These projects involve complex workflows, financial calculations, regulatory requirements, multiple users and significant responsibilities around data protection.

They're ambitious, and I make no secret of that.

One of my projects has also led to my first offer of paid software-development work.

That was an extraordinary moment for me.

In October 2026, I established EnantiomorphAI, my own sole-trader development business.

What started as curiosity has become a genuine new professional opportunity.

And I already have ideas for further applications once the current projects are sufficiently developed.

## Discovering Codex

My development journey has involved both ChatGPT and Codex.

ChatGPT has helped me explore ideas, challenge assumptions, define requirements and reason through complex processes.

Codex has helped me work on the software itself, including implementing changes, examining code, testing functionality and assessing technical readiness.

Interestingly, I didn't initially appreciate just how much Codex was contributing to my development workflow.

In one recent property-management task, Codex worked across multiple files and produced a technical-readiness assessment identifying completed work, outstanding requirements and areas requiring further attention.

That helped me recognise an important distinction.

Getting software to work is not the same as knowing that it works correctly.

## From prototype to production: the challenge that matters most

This is where my previous experience commissioning software becomes particularly relevant.

I've worked on development projects where everything appeared to function correctly during testing, only for significant problems to emerge when the system went live.

That's something I'm determined not to repeat.

My property-management system will eventually be used by staff who depend on it to carry out their daily responsibilities.

They need to trust it.

A system that looks impressive but produces unreliable financial information, fails to handle exceptions or disrupts established processes isn't a successful system.

So one of my biggest challenges now is learning how to move from working prototypes to dependable production software.

How do I test an entire workflow rather than an isolated function? How do I know that a change in one area hasn't broken something elsewhere? How do I establish meaningful acceptance criteria? And when should I bring in experienced engineers to independently review what I've built?

These are questions I'm actively exploring.

I'm discovering that AI-native development isn't simply about generating code. It's about understanding requirements, anticipating failures, verifying results and recognising the limits of your own expertise.

## A new kind of developer?

I've encountered the term AI-native developer, and it resonates with me.

I don't have a traditional software-engineering background, and I wouldn't claim the experience of someone who has spent decades developing and maintaining production systems.

But I am developing software.

I define requirements, design workflows, test functionality, investigate problems and make decisions about how applications should behave.

AI is central to that process from the outset.

And it makes me wonder how many other professionals have ideas for useful applications but have never considered themselves capable of building them.

People working in healthcare, education, law, finance, manufacturing, public services and countless other industries possess specialist knowledge that could become the foundation for valuable software.

What happens when those people gain the tools to explore their ideas themselves?

## Why I'm sharing this journey

I'm genuinely excited about what I've achieved so far, and even more excited about what might come next.

There's an enormous amount I still need to learn. I expect mistakes, unexpected challenges and discoveries along the way.

That's precisely what I want to document.

Through EnantiomorphAI, I'll share my experiences of working with ChatGPT and Codex, developing increasingly complex applications and learning how to turn promising prototypes into dependable software.

I'll explore the wider possibilities of AI for professionals from all industries, while protecting the confidential information and intellectual property associated with my projects.

I want this to be a place for curiosity, honest discussion and learning from other developers.

I'm not interested in pretending to have all the answers.

I'm interested in discovering what's possible.

And right now, the possibilities feel extraordinary.

EnantiomorphAI — Curious by nature. Building with intelligence.
